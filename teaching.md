## Teaching Philosophy

### Integrate Humanities and Social Sciences
I believe engineering education must incorporate social context and ethical judgement to create responsible engineers. This philosophy is rooted in my time as a student where learning to tackle ethical and social issues as they related to science and engineering ingrained the importance of seeing the bigger picture. I carry this perspective into my own teaching by structuring lessons around integration, hands-on learning, and discussion. 

When I was a TA mentoring capstone design teams, I helped students integrate content from their coursework, apply it to their projects, and consider human and societal impacts of their projects. One team of students, who were building a robotic exoskeleton, was solely motivated by the engineering rigor of the project and lacked a documented community need. I challenged them to consider whether they were designing _for_ or _with_ their target population and contrasted the goal of 'fixing' an impairment with co-creating tools for autonomy. While the students were initially resistant to moving away from their original vision, our discussions ultimately led them to shift their scope from a personal device to an in-clinic tool. This change allowed them to focus on improving an existing clinical process, resulting in a design that restored user autonomy during therapy by reducing the need for constant, hands-on therapist intervention and ensuring the device remained accessible through insurance-supported care.

### Foster Professional Skills 
Beyond course content, instructors are also responsible for fostering professional skills and a supportive environment. While many degrees have a communications or professional development course as a major-requirement,  developing these skills takes more practice and repetition than can be achieved in just one semester. As an instructor I will adopt class-wide mentoring, or short presentations, throughout my courses that address topics that I wish I was taught as a student. Some skills are especially crucial for first-year students transitioning to college, like learning how to study or access campus resources like tutoring. As stusents progress in their degrees, the skill-focus should shift to cover summer options (e.g., research, internships) and post-graduation paths. 

### Prioritize an Inclusive Classroom
To create an inclusive classroom that supports all learners, I plan on implementing Universal Design for Learning (UDL) to help reduce the most common barriers for students. This includes providing notes and lecture recordings, offering flexibility in assessment types, and maintaining compassionate policies for absences or extensions. By proactively implementing these accommodations, I reduce the burden on students to constantly advocate for their basic needs. Team-based work can be particularly challenging as it requires navigating interpersonal dynamics in conjunction with learning and implementing new course content. To address this, I have found success in utilizing team contracts to provide a reference point for students, TAs, and instructors to consult throughout the semester. 

### Vocally Support Students
I believe that faculty directly and vocally supporting students is a fundamental part of inclusive education. When I lost two family members within a week during my senior year, an engineering professor worked with me
to adjust my assignments to ensure I was taking care of my mental health and could still be successful in the class. As a student, I deeply valued the compassion she had for me and now, it has informed my
standard for mentorship. Compassion is a fundamental component of academic persistence, and faculty need to offer holistic support to their students. 

### Celebrate Diversity 
Incorporating diverse examples into curricula can get students excited about engineering and support a sense of belonging, which is a significant factor in the attrition rates of minoritized students. Some fun engineering examples include the Black women who worked as computers for NASA and are responsible for the moon landing, and Emily Roebling, who took over as the lead engineer on the Brooklyn Bridge when her husband fell ill.
